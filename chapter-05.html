V. Conclusion: Subversive History, Bright Future

"Someone asked me the other day what it feels like to see all my 'old stuff' reappearing, at long last, in digital. And I had to smile because to me it doesn't feel like 'old stuff.'"
- Barbara Hambly

The Importance of Shatter as Media Art

Shatter is very much of its time. Its computer-generated art from the mid-1980s is stiff and lifeless. At the same time, it shows the mad energy of artists discovering that a piece of equipment can do more than anyone before them had thought. This energy may have been fueled by the intention to subvert the traditional comics production process.

The art style is primitive by today's standards, but next to the run-of-the-mill story it is most definitely the star of the series. It is still rewarding to look at each page and marvel at the intricacies that Mike Saenz and Charlie Athanas were able to eke out of their Macintoshes.

While only modestly entertaining as a commercial product, Shatter is a cultural artifact and a relic of a long-bygone era, with a good helping of quirkiness and originality in its execution. It marks an evolutionary step on the way to where technology has brought us today. To stay with the metaphor, this comic book is the fish whose fins already resemble legs, even though it has not yet fully left the sea. The idea behind Shatter is fascinating: that one day comics might be produced digitally. But in 1985, Mike Saenz and Peter B. Gillis (and later Charlie Athanas) were impatient and did not want to wait for technology to catch up with their aspirations.

Archiving Shatter for Media Arts Histories

Even though Shatter was produced digitally, it was always meant for print. Its creators and its publisher set out to print it and distribute it as a physical product. Preserving individual printed copies follows the same principles as preserving other print media and periodicals: archives with regulated temperature and humidity and a complete absence of light during storage.

Aside from the physical medium, Shatter is one of the earliest artifacts whose raw content exists as digital files. Unfortunately, the demise of First Comics Publishing makes it unlikely that the original files in MacPaint format can be obtained by this route. It is certainly possible to seek them from the original artists, but the media they are stored on (floppy disks or hard disks) might be corrupted after three decades of storage. If the files can be found, they can be archived in distributed storage systems such as a cloud or a RAID setup. In one of the letters columns, the editor notes that for a small handling fee the publishing company would be prepared to send a disk with digital artwork to interested parties. If any readers took up the offer, digital copies might be stored away on disks somewhere in the United States.

Apart from the files generated during production, there might even be an animated digital trailer. According to one source, the original artist put together something of the kind, which was distributed on a disk[15]. These might also still be available.

Locating them is beyond the scope of this paper.

Learning from Shatter

One might argue that it does not matter whether Shatter is a good piece of entertainment, an accomplished piece of art, or the culmination of a cultural discipline. Even at the time of its creation, it never pretended or aspired to be any of these. To many, Shatter exerts the same strange fascination as Eadweard Muybridge's Victorian-era sequential photographs of galloping horses and walking men and women, even though we now have 4K footage of the same subjects in much higher quality.

It can even be argued that Shatter has not had a great influence on how we create and read comics today; its reach was not great enough for that. But the technology it employed had certainly taken its first steps towards the total digitization of the visual arts. Today we are used to seeing computer-generated graphics in film, in commercial art, and in comics. The creators of Shatter were the first to see these possibilities in the available hardware, before it was really capable enough.

When we run original Macintosh software such as MacPaint, MacDraw, and FullPaint in emulation, it is easy to lose sight of the context in which it was used at the time. Perhaps Shatter can open our eyes to this context. We should not frown upon the software's limitations but recognize that artists used it for actual, honest-to-God production work. They saw the potential for experimentation rather than the restrictions, and all this with publication deadlines looming.

Exploring Shatter shows us the achievements of its creators as well as their omissions, failings, and blind spots. They made no use of the nascent 3D graphics already taking shape on the 16-bit machines of the time. In the year of Shatter's cancellation, the German comics artist Michael Götze was already using 3D graphics in his electronic comic Das Robot Imperium (Carlsen Comics, 1988) on the Atari ST, a 16-bit computer of a similar generation to the Apple Macintosh.

It is easy to take Shatter at face value and see only its clunkiness, its deficiencies, and the ineptitude of its execution. As an audience, and perhaps even as fellow artists, we can instead look at Shatter and ask ourselves how much time and effort Mike Saenz and Charlie Athanas put into the work. At any point, they could have given up and abandoned this tedious and thankless exercise. Perhaps each of them at some point stepped back from the nine-inch screen with its monochrome line art and shook his head at how much effort he had put into work that to some audiences might look bad or even, as Mike Gold put it, like "some amphetamine addict had been given a box of a zip-a-tone." Maybe they thought how much easier it would have been to take pencil and pen to paper instead.

And perhaps they knew how "cool" they were, and perhaps they felt how subversive their art was because they were at the technological razor's edge in a world not yet ready for it.
